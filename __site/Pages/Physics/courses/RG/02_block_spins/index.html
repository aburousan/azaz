    Renormalisation Group II — Block Spins

    Part 2 of seven. previous: Why scale matters · Course home · next: Fixed points & universality

    Part 1 argued that the parameters of a theory depend on the resolution you describe it at. Now we make that concrete in a system small enough to solve exactly, with no field theory in sight.

    The Ising model in one paragraph

    Put a spin \(s_i=\pm1\) (up or down) on each site of a lattice. Neighbouring spins like to point the same way, so the energy is

    \[ E = -J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle} s_i s_j , \]

    summed over nearest-neighbour pairs, with \(J>0\). At temperature \(T\) each configuration has probability proportional to \(e^{-E/k_BT}\). Only the combination \(K\equiv J/k_BT\) ever appears, so we call it the coupling. Large \(K\) means cold, and the spins align. Small \(K\) means hot, and they're random. Everything is encoded in the partition function

    \[ Z(K) = \sum_{\{s\}} \exp\Big(K\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j\Big). \]

    For QFT readers, this is a path integral. The "field" is \(s_i\), the "action" is \(-K\sum s_is_j\), and the sum over configurations is the integral over field histories. Coarse-graining the Ising model is the lattice version of integrating out short-distance modes.

    Kadanoff's idea

    In 1966 Leo Kadanoff made a simple observation. Near a critical point, spins are correlated over huge distances, so a small block of neighbouring spins mostly points one way. Replace each block by a single "block spin" that points the way the majority does. You now have a lattice with fewer spins and a larger spacing that looks like another Ising model, only with a different coupling \(K'\).

    Majority-rule blocking. Each 3×3 block (dashed) is replaced by one spin pointing the way most of its members point. Do it again and you have one spin. Each round loses detail, and the question is what happens to the coupling between the surviving spins.

    If blocking maps the model to itself with a new coupling, we get a function \(K' = R(K)\), the RG map. Iterating it tells us how the system looks at larger and larger scales. In two and three dimensions majority rule can only be done approximately. In one dimension a closely related trick can be done exactly, so that's where we start.

    Decimating the chain

    Take an infinite chain with coupling \(K\) between neighbours. Instead of majority rule, use decimation: sum over every second spin and keep the others. Look at one decimated spin \(s_2\), sitting between two survivors \(s_1\) and \(s_3\). It appears in exactly two bonds, so

    \[ \sum_{s_2=\pm1} e^{K s_2 (s_1+s_3)} = 2\cosh\big(K(s_1+s_3)\big). \]

    Summing over the even spins leaves a chain of odd spins, twice as far apart, joined by a new coupling \(K'\).

    We want this to look like a bond of the same form, \(f\,e^{K's_1s_3}\), for some new coupling \(K'\) and some constant \(f\). There are only two cases to match:

    \[ s_1=s_3:\quad 2\cosh 2K = f\,e^{K'}, \qquad\qquad s_1=-s_3:\quad 2 = f\,e^{-K'}. \]

    Two equations, two unknowns. Dividing them gives \(e^{2K'}=\cosh 2K\), and multiplying gives \(f^2=4\cosh 2K\):

    \[ \boxed{\;K' = \tfrac12\ln\cosh 2K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tanh K' = \tanh^2 K, \qquad f(K) = 2\sqrt{\cosh 2K}.\;} \]

    The two forms of \(K'\) are equivalent because \(\tanh K' = (e^{2K'}-1)/(e^{2K'}+1) = (\cosh2K-1)/(\cosh2K+1) = \sinh^2K/\cosh^2K\).

    Nothing was approximated. The partition function of \(N\) spins at coupling \(K\) equals a known factor times the partition function of \(N/2\) spins at coupling \(K'\):

    \[ Z_N(K) = f(K)^{N/2}\, Z_{N/2}(K'). \]

    That's a complete, exact RG step. Here is the check in Wolfram Language (it runs as printed):

    dec[s1_, s3_] := Exp[K (s1 + s3)] + Exp[-K (s1 + s3)];      (* sum over s2 *)
sol = Solve[{dec[1, 1] == f Exp[Kp], dec[1, -1] == f Exp[-Kp]}, {f, Kp}];
Kp1 = Kp /. First[sol];
FullSimplify[Kp1 - Log[Cosh[2 K]]/2, K > 0]           (* -> 0 *)
FullSimplify[Tanh[Log[Cosh[2 K]]/2] - Tanh[K]^2, K > 0] (* -> 0 *)
FullSimplify[f /. First[sol], K > 0]                   (* -> 2 Sqrt[Cosh[2K]] *)

    Following the flow

    Now iterate: \(K\to K'\to K''\to\cdots\). Since \(0\le\tanh K<1\), squaring always makes it smaller, so \(K\) decreases at every step. Two limits tell the whole story:

    • For small \(K\), \(K' = K^2 - \tfrac23K^4+\dots\). It collapses to zero very fast.

    • For large \(K\), \(K' = K - \tfrac12\ln 2 + (\text{exponentially small})\). It drifts down slowly, by \(0.347\) per step.

    Left: the map \(K'=R(K)\) lies below the diagonal everywhere, so iterating it (red staircase) always walks down to \(K=0\). Right: the same information drawn as a flow on the line of couplings. There are two fixed points, \(K^*=0\) and \(K^*=\infty\). Every finite starting point is attracted to the first.

    🧠 Defn A fixed point of the RG is a value of the couplings that the map leaves unchanged: \(R(K^*)=K^*\). A system sitting exactly at a fixed point looks the same at every scale.

    The two fixed points of the chain are the two ends of the temperature axis. At \(K^*=0\) (infinite temperature) the spins are independent and every scale looks like random noise. At \(K^*=\infty\) (zero temperature) they're perfectly aligned and every scale looks uniform. Both are trivially scale-invariant. The flow says that any chain at finite temperature, looked at from far enough away, looks like the infinite-temperature one: disordered. That is the RG way of saying the 1D Ising model has no phase transition, a fact Ising proved by brute force in 1925.

    The correlation length, and a small miracle

    How far does order extend? The spin–spin correlation in the chain is exactly \(\langle s_0s_r\rangle = (\tanh K)^r = e^{-r/\xi}\), which defines the correlation length in lattice units:

    \[ \xi(K) = -\frac{1}{\ln\tanh K}. \]

    After one decimation the lattice spacing has doubled. A physical distance that used to be \(2r\) steps is now \(r\) steps, so if the RG is doing its job, the correlation length measured in the new units must be half the old one: \(\xi(K')=\xi(K)/2\). Check it: \(\ln\tanh K' = \ln\tanh^2 K = 2\ln\tanh K\), so \(\xi(K')=\xi(K)/2\)exactly.

    This is the most important equation in the course, in its simplest form. The RG throws away detail but keeps physics. Lengths measured in the shrinking units of the coarse lattice shrink by the scale factor \(b\) at each step:

    \[ \xi(K') = \xi(K)/b. \]

    At a fixed point \(K'=K\), so \(\xi=\xi/b\). That leaves only \(\xi=0\) or \(\xi=\infty\). A fixed point has either no correlations or infinitely long-ranged ones. The interesting fixed points, the ones behind phase transitions, are the second kind. The chain doesn't have one at finite \(K\); the next part finds one in two dimensions.

    Exercises

    🤔 Problem 2.1. Near \(K=\infty\) write \(w=e^{-2K}\). Show that one decimation gives \(w'\approx2w\). How does \(\xi\) depend on \(K\) at low temperature, and is that consistent with the exact formula?
    Show solution
    From \(K'\approx K-\tfrac12\ln2\) we get \(e^{-2K'}=2e^{-2K}\), so \(w'=2w\). Doubling the length scale doubles \(w\). Since \(\xi\) must halve when \(w\) doubles, \(\xi\propto w^{-1}=e^{2K}\). Exactly: \(\tanh K = 1-2e^{-2K}+\dots\), so \(\ln\tanh K\approx-2e^{-2K}\) and \(\xi\approx\tfrac12e^{2K}\). At \(K=3\) the exact value is \(201.714\) and \(\tfrac12e^{6}=201.714\). The correlation length diverges exponentially as \(T\to0\), not as a power. That's another sign there's no ordinary critical point.

    🤔 Problem 2.2. Free energy from the flow. Let \(g(K)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\ln Z_N(K)\). Use \(Z_N(K)=f(K)^{N/2}Z_{N/2}(K')\) to show

    \[ g(K) = \tfrac12\ln f(K) + \tfrac12 g(K'). \]

    Iterate it, using \(g(0)=\ln2\), and check numerically that you recover the exact answer \(g(K)=\ln(2\cosh K)\).

    Show solution
    Take the log and divide by \(N\): \(\frac1N\ln Z_N = \frac12\ln f + \frac12\cdot\frac{1}{N/2}\ln Z_{N/2}(K')\). Iterating, \(g(K)=\sum_{n\ge0}2^{-(n+1)}\ln f(K_n)+\lim_n 2^{-n}g(K_n)\), and since \(K_n\to0\) the last term vanishes. Summing 60 terms numerically gives \(g=0.737487950486\) at \(K=0.3\), \(1.126928011043\) at \(K=1\), and \(2.506715348489\) at \(K=2.5\), agreeing with \(\ln(2\cosh K)\) to all twelve digits. The RG didn't just give the flow of \(K\). The constants \(f\) we kept track of add up to the full thermodynamics. That's typical: the "boring" constant in each step carries the free energy.

    🤔 Problem 2.3. Add a magnetic field, \(E=-J\sum s_is_{i+1}-H\sum s_i\), and write \(h=H/k_BT\). Decimate again. You now need three constants \((f,K',h')\) and there are three independent cases \((s_1,s_3)=(+,+),(-,-),(+,-)\). Show that

    \[ e^{4K'} = \frac{\cosh(2K+h)\cosh(2K-h)}{\cosh^2 h}, \qquad e^{2h'} = e^{2h}\,\frac{\cosh(2K+h)}{\cosh(2K-h)}. \]

    (Longer.) Where are the fixed points in the \((K,h)\) plane?

    Show solution
    Split the field on each survivor half-and-half between its two bonds, so each surviving bond carries \(e^{Ks_1s_3+\frac h2(s_1+s_3)}\) in the new chain. The sum over \(s_2\) gives \(e^{\frac h2(s_1+s_3)}\,2\cosh(K(s_1+s_3)+h)\). Match \(f\,e^{K's_1s_3+\frac{h'}2(s_1+s_3)}\): \((+,+)\): \(f e^{K'+h'} = 2e^{h}\cosh(2K+h)\); \((-,-)\): \(f e^{K'-h'} = 2e^{-h}\cosh(2K-h)\); \((+,-)\): \(f e^{-K'} = 2\cosh h\). The ratio of the first two gives \(e^{2h'}\). The product of the first two divided by the square of the third gives \(e^{4K'}\). At \(h=0\) these reduce to \(e^{2K'}=\cosh2K\), as they should. The fixed points are the line \(K^*=0\) (any \(h\), since independent spins in a field are scale-invariant) and the point \(K^*=\infty,\ h^*=0\). The field is relevant there: \(h'>h\) for \(K\) large, so \(h\) grows under the flow. We meet that word properly in Part 3.
