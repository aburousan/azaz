    Renormalisation Group V — The Wilson–Fisher Fixed Point

    Part 5 of seven. previous: Wilson's shells · Course home · next: Running couplings

    In Part 4 the free field gave us the Gaussian fixed point, and power counting showed that below four dimensions the quartic coupling destabilises it. Now we turn \(u\) on and do step 1 of the RG, integrating out the shell, for real. It's a one-loop calculation, and it's the calculation that earned Wilson his Nobel prize.

    Integrating out the shell

    Split the interaction using \(\phi=\phi_<+\phi_>\):

    \[ \frac u{4!}\int(\phi_<+\phi_>)^4 = \frac u{4!}\int\Big(\phi_<^4 + 4\phi_<^3\phi_> + 6\phi_<^2\phi_>^2 + 4\phi_<\phi_>^3 + \phi_>^4\Big). \]

    Treat \(S_{\rm int}\) as small, and do the \(\phi_>\) integral with the free Gaussian weight of the fast modes, whose propagator is \(G(k)=1/(k^2+r)\) for \(k\) in the shell. The standard cumulant expansion of \(\int\mathcal D\phi_>e^{-S_0-S_{\rm int}}\) gives

    \[ S'[\phi_<] = S_0[\phi_<] + \langle S_{\rm int}\rangle_> - \tfrac12\Big(\langle S_{\rm int}^2\rangle_> - \langle S_{\rm int}\rangle_>^2\Big) + \dots \]

    where \(\langle\cdot\rangle_>\) averages over fast modes only. Each term is a set of Feynman diagrams, with fast propagators on the internal lines and slow fields on the external legs.

    The two one-loop diagrams, with only fast (shell) modes running round the loop. The tadpole renormalises the mass; the bubble renormalises the coupling.

    The mass. From \(\langle S_{\rm int}\rangle_>\), take the \(6\phi_<^2\phi_>^2\) term and contract the two fast fields: \(\langle\phi_>^2\rangle=\int_{\rm shell}G\). That gives \(\frac{u}{4!}\cdot6\cdot\int_{\rm shell}G\,\int\phi_<^2\). Comparing with \(\tfrac12\delta r\int\phi_<^2\):

    \[ \delta r = \frac u2\int_{\rm shell}\frac{\mathrm d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}\,\frac1{k^2+r}. \]

    The coupling. From \(-\tfrac12\langle S_{\rm int}^2\rangle_c\), take two vertices, keep two slow legs on each, and join them with two fast propagators. Count the ways: choose which 2 of the 4 legs at each vertex are slow, \(\binom42^2=36\), then join the two fast legs at vertex 1 to the two at vertex 2 in \(2\) ways. That's \(72\) in all. So the term is \(-\tfrac12\left(\frac u{4!}\right)^2\cdot72\int_{\rm shell}G^2\int\phi_<^4 = -\frac{u^2}{16}\int_{\rm shell}G^2\int\phi_<^4\), evaluated at zero external momentum, which is fine for the local \(\phi^4\) term. Matching to \(\frac{\delta u}{4!}\int\phi_<^4\):

    \[ \delta u = -\frac{3u^2}{2}\int_{\rm shell}\frac{\mathrm d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}\,\frac1{(k^2+r)^2}. \]

    The minus sign matters: fluctuations weaken the coupling. You'll see in Part 6 that this coefficient, \(3/2\), is exactly what's needed for the field-theory beta function, an independent check on the counting.

    The shell integral. For a thin shell, \(b=e^{\delta\ell}\) with \(\delta\ell\ll1\), the integrand is constant across the shell:

    \[ \int_{\Lambda/b}^{\Lambda}\frac{\mathrm d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}F(k) = K_d\,\Lambda^d\,F(\Lambda)\,\delta\ell,\qquad K_d=\frac{S_d}{(2\pi)^d}=\frac{2}{(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2)}. \]

    \(K_2=1/2\pi\), \(K_3=1/2\pi^2\), \(K_4=1/8\pi^2\).

    The flow equations

    Now do steps 2 and 3, the rescaling from Part 4, which multiplies \(r\) by \(b^2\) and \(u\) by \(b^{4-d}\) (at one loop the field rescaling is unchanged, since no \(k^2\phi^2\) term is generated). Work with dimensionless couplings, \(\tilde r=r/\Lambda^2\) and \(\tilde u=u\Lambda^{d-4}\), and let \(\delta\ell\to0\). The result is a pair of differential equations, with \(\ell\) the "RG time" running towards the infrared:

    \[ \boxed{\;\frac{\mathrm d\tilde r}{\mathrm d\ell} = 2\tilde r + \frac{K_d}{2}\,\frac{\tilde u}{1+\tilde r},\qquad \frac{\mathrm d\tilde u}{\mathrm d\ell} = (4-d)\,\tilde u - \frac{3K_d}{2}\,\frac{\tilde u^2}{(1+\tilde r)^2}.\;} \]

    Read them term by term. The first term of each is Part 4's tree-level scaling. The second terms are the fluctuations we just integrated out. The quartic coupling feeds the mass (the tadpole), and it screens itself (the bubble).

    These are the same equations as in Melo's notes (their \(\beta_2,\beta_4\)), once you flip the sign of the RG time.

    The fixed points

    Set \(\epsilon=4-d\) and look for zeros. Since the fixed point turns out to be at \(\tilde u=O(\epsilon)\) and \(\tilde r=O(\epsilon)\), to leading order we can drop \(\tilde r\) in the denominators and set \(K_d\to K_4=1/8\pi^2\):

    • Gaussian: \(\tilde r^*=\tilde u^*=0\).

    • Wilson–Fisher: \(\epsilon\tilde u^*=\frac{3K_4}{2}\tilde u^{*2}\), giving

    \[ \boxed{\;\tilde u^* = \frac{2\epsilon}{3K_4}=\frac{16\pi^2}{3}\,\epsilon,\qquad \tilde r^* = -\frac{K_4\tilde u^*}{4} = -\frac\epsilon6.\;} \]

    For \(\epsilon>0\) the new fixed point sits at positive coupling, which is where a physical (stable) \(\phi^4\) theory lives. For \(\epsilon\to0\) it merges with the Gaussian one.

    Left: the right-hand side of the \(\tilde u\) equation. In \(d=4\) (black) it's negative for all \(\tilde u>0\), so the coupling always decreases towards the infrared and the only fixed point is \(\tilde u=0\). For \(\epsilon>0\) the parabola crosses zero again, and the crossing is the Wilson–Fisher point. Right: trajectories of the full one-loop flow in \(d=3\), integrated numerically. The critical surface (blue) runs into the Wilson–Fisher point.

    Exponents

    Linearise around Wilson–Fisher. To first order in \(\epsilon\) the stability matrix is triangular (exercise 5.2), with eigenvalues

    \[ y_t = 2-\frac\epsilon3, \qquad y_u=-\epsilon. \]

    One relevant direction (temperature) and one irrelevant one, just as a critical point should have. The Gaussian point has lost the fight: there both eigenvalues are positive (\(2\) and \(+\epsilon\)), so it can't be reached by tuning one knob. Every critical Ising system in \(d<4\) flows to Wilson–Fisher. The exponents follow:

    \[ \nu = \frac1{y_t} = \frac12+\frac\epsilon{12}+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad \gamma = \nu(2-\eta) = 1+\frac\epsilon6+O(\epsilon^2), \]

    since \(\eta\) first appears at two loops, \(\eta=\epsilon^2/54\). Everything above was checked in Wolfram:

    betam = 2 m2 + Kd4 u/(2 (1 + m2));
betau = eps u - 3 Kd4 u^2/(2 (1 + m2)^2);
us  = u /. Last[Solve[eps u - 3 Kd4 u^2/2 == 0, u]];        (* 2 eps/(3 Kd4) *)
m2s = m2 /. First[Solve[2 m2 + Kd4 us/2 == 0, m2]];        (* -eps/6 *)
yt  = Simplify[D[betam, m2] /. {m2 -> 0, u -> us}]          (* 2 - eps/3 *)
Series[1/yt, {eps, 0, 1}]                                   (* 1/2 + eps/12 *)
Simplify[D[betau, u] /. {m2 -> 0, u -> us}]                 (* -eps *)
Simplify[2/((4 Pi)^(4/2) Gamma[4/2]) - 1/(8 Pi^2)]          (* 0: K_4 = 1/(8 Pi^2) *)

    How good is it?

    Set \(\epsilon=1\) for three dimensions. That's not small, and the expansion has no business working. Still:

    \(\nu\)\(\gamma\)
    mean field (Gaussian)0.51
    one loop, \(\epsilon=1\)0.5831.167
    3D Ising, conformal bootstrap0.63001.2371

    The one-loop \(\nu\) against \(\epsilon\). It moves from mean field in the right direction and gets most of the way to the true 3D value. At \(\epsilon=2\) (the 2D Ising model) it's far off, as it should be. Higher orders, plus resummation, bring the 3D numbers within a fraction of a percent.

    The first-order correction takes \(\nu\) two-thirds of the way from mean field to the true value. The reason this works is that the ε expansion is organised by the fixed point's structure, not by the size of the coupling. The deeper point is qualitative, and it's exact: below four dimensions the exponents are not mean-field, they are universal, and they are fixed by a single number \(\epsilon\) plus the symmetry.

    Exercises

    🤔 Problem 5.1. Redo the counting of the bubble term, but for the tadpole: show that \(\langle S_{\rm int}\rangle_>\) contributes \(\frac u4\langle\phi_>^2\rangle\int\phi_<^2\) and hence \(\delta r=\frac u2\int_{\rm shell}G\).
    Show solution
    The \(\phi_<^2\phi_>^2\) term appears with coefficient \(\binom42=6\) in the expansion of \((\phi_<+\phi_>)^4\). Averaging replaces \(\phi_>^2\) by \(\langle\phi_>^2\rangle=\int_{\rm shell}\frac{\mathrm d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}G(k)\). So the term is \(\frac u{24}\cdot6\langle\phi_>^2\rangle\int\phi_<^2=\frac u4\langle\phi_>^2\rangle\int\phi_<^2\). Setting that equal to \(\frac12\delta r\int\phi_<^2\) gives \(\delta r=\frac u2\langle\phi_>^2\rangle\). (The \(\phi_<^3\phi_>\) and \(\phi_<\phi_>^3\) terms average to zero, since they have an odd number of fast fields.)

    🤔 Problem 5.2. Compute the stability matrix \(M_{ij}=\partial(\mathrm d g_i/\mathrm d\ell)/\partial g_j\) at the Wilson–Fisher point, to first order in \(\epsilon\), and show its eigenvalues are \(2-\epsilon/3\) and \(-\epsilon\).
    Show solution
    \(\partial_r\dot r = 2-\frac{K_4\tilde u^*}{2(1+\tilde r^*)^2}\approx2-\frac\epsilon3\). \(\partial_u\dot r=\frac{K_4}{2}\). \(\partial_r\dot u = 3K_4\tilde u^{*2}/(1+\tilde r^*)^3 = O(\epsilon^2)\), so drop it. \(\partial_u\dot u = \epsilon-3K_4\tilde u^*=\epsilon-2\epsilon=-\epsilon\). To this order the matrix is upper triangular, and its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries: \(2-\epsilon/3\) and \(-\epsilon\). Melo's notes get the same, \(-2+\epsilon/3\) and \(\epsilon\), with the opposite sign convention for RG time.

    🤔 Problem 5.3. The O(N) model. Let \(\boldsymbol\phi\) have \(N\) components and interaction \(\frac u{4!}(\boldsymbol\phi\cdot\boldsymbol\phi)^2\). The combinatorics change: the tadpole factor becomes \(\frac{N+2}{6}u\) (was \(\frac12u\)) and the bubble factor \(\frac{N+8}{6}u^2\) (was \(\frac32u^2\)). Find \(\tilde u^*\) and \(\nu\) to first order in \(\epsilon\). Check that \(N=1\) gives the Ising result, and find \(\nu\) as \(N\to\infty\). (Longer.)
    Show solution
    \(\dot{\tilde u}=\epsilon\tilde u-\frac{N+8}6K_4\tilde u^2\), so \(\tilde u^*=\frac{6\epsilon}{(N+8)K_4}\). Then \(y_t=2-\frac{N+2}6K_4\tilde u^*=2-\frac{N+2}{N+8}\epsilon\) and \(\nu=\frac12+\frac{N+2}{4(N+8)}\epsilon\). For \(N=1\) that's \(\frac12+\frac{3}{36}\epsilon=\frac12+\frac\epsilon{12}\). As \(N\to\infty\), \(y_t\to2-\epsilon=d-2\), so \(\nu=1/(d-2)\), which is the exact answer for the large-\(N\) (spherical) model. At \(\epsilon=1\): XY magnets and superfluid helium (\(N=2\)) get \(0.600\) against a measured \(0.6717\), and Heisenberg magnets (\(N=3\)) get \(0.614\) against \(0.711\). Different \(N\) means a different universality class: the symmetry of the order parameter is one of the few things the fixed point remembers.

    🤔 Problem 5.4. In \(d=4\) exactly, solve \(\mathrm d\tilde u/\mathrm d\ell=-\frac{3}{16\pi^2}\tilde u^2\) starting from \(\tilde u_0\). How does the coupling behave at large \(\ell\) (the infrared)? Run backwards to the ultraviolet, \(\ell\to-\infty\). What goes wrong?
    Show solution
    Separate variables: \(\frac{1}{\tilde u(\ell)}=\frac1{\tilde u_0}+\frac{3\ell}{16\pi^2}\). In the infrared (\(\ell\to\infty\)) the coupling goes to zero like \(\frac{16\pi^2}{3\ell}\): marginally irrelevant, so the theory becomes free at long distance, but only logarithmically. Towards the ultraviolet (\(\ell<0\)) the denominator vanishes at \(\ell=-\frac{16\pi^2}{3\tilde u_0}\): the coupling blows up at a finite scale, the Landau pole. We meet this again in Part 6, as a statement about QED, and in Part 7, as "triviality".
