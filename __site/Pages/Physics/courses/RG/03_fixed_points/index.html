    Renormalisation Group III — Fixed Points and Universality

    Part 3 of seven. previous: Block spins · Course home · next: Wilson's shells

    The 1D chain had only trivial fixed points, because it has no phase transition. In two and three dimensions the Ising model does order below a critical temperature \(T_c\), and at \(T_c\) something remarkable happens. This part is about what.

    What a critical point looks like

    Three snapshots of a 256×256 Ising model, sampled with the Wolff cluster algorithm. Hot: small islands, short correlations. Cold: one colour wins. Critical: islands inside lakes inside islands, at every size up to the box itself.

    At \(T_c\) the correlation length diverges. Measured near \(T_c\) it follows a power law,

    \[ \xi \propto |T-T_c|^{-\nu}, \]

    and similarly the specific heat goes like \(|T-T_c|^{-\alpha}\), the magnetisation below \(T_c\) like \((T_c-T)^{\beta}\), and the susceptibility like \(|T-T_c|^{-\gamma}\). The numbers \(\nu,\alpha,\beta,\gamma\) are the critical exponents. Two facts about them were deeply puzzling before the RG:

    1. They are often not simple fractions. For the 3D Ising model \(\nu=0.6300\) and \(\beta=0.3265\). Nothing in the Hamiltonian looks like either number.

    2. They are universal. A uniaxial magnet, the liquid–gas critical point of water or xenon, and a binary alloy ordering on its lattice all share the same exponents, although the microscopic physics has nothing in common.

    Both facts come out of one picture.

    Why the critical point is a fixed point

    Recall the rule from Part 2: one RG step with scale factor \(b\) maps the couplings \(K\to K'\) and shrinks the correlation length in lattice units, \(\xi(K')=\xi(K)/b\). At \(T_c\), \(\xi=\infty\), and \(\infty/b=\infty\), so the critical point flows to a point with infinite correlation length, which has to be a fixed point \(K^*\). And the fixed point can't be at \(K=0\) or \(K=\infty\), where \(\xi=0\). It is a genuinely new, nontrivial fixed point.

    You can see this with your own eyes. Block-spin the snapshots:

    Three rounds of 2×2 majority-rule blocking. Above \(T_c\) (top) the picture flows towards noise, the \(K=0\) fixed point. Below \(T_c\) (bottom) it flows to a uniform state, the \(K=\infty\) fixed point. At \(T_c\) (middle) it stays statistically the same. That is the nontrivial fixed point.

    Now do it live. The simulation below runs in your browser.

    Set \(T/T_c=1.05\) and watch: the original panel still looks critical-ish, but by the third blocking the noise has won. Move to \(0.97\) and the opposite happens. The closer you get to \(T_c\), the more blocking steps it takes to tell which way the flow is going. That delay is a diverging correlation length, seen from the RG side.

    Linearising the flow

    Near a fixed point, small deviations grow or shrink geometrically. With one coupling, write \(K=K^*+\delta K\) and expand the RG map to first order:

    \[ \delta K' = \Lambda\,\delta K, \qquad \Lambda = R'(K^*) \equiv b^{\,y}. \]

    The exponent \(y\) is defined so that it doesn't depend on the arbitrary step size \(b\) (two steps of \(b\) must equal one step of \(b^2\)). Three cases:

    🧠 Defn

    • \(y>0\): \(\delta K\)grows under the flow. The coupling is relevant. Any small deviation from the fixed point is amplified at large scales.

    • \(y<0\): \(\delta K\)shrinks. The coupling is irrelevant. Its effect is washed out at large scales.

    • \(y=0\): marginal. Linear analysis can't decide, and you need the next order (this case is the drama of Parts 5 and 6).

    Temperature must be relevant at a critical point, because being slightly off \(T_c\) is exactly what makes the system flow away. Call \(t=(T-T_c)/T_c\), and let its eigenvalue be \(y_t\). Then:

    \[ \xi(t) = b\,\xi(b^{y_t}t). \]

    This holds for any \(b\), so choose \(b=|t|^{-1/y_t}\) so that the argument on the right is \(\pm1\):

    \[ \xi(t) = |t|^{-1/y_t}\,\xi(\pm1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;\nu = \frac1{y_t}.\;} \]

    A critical exponent is the inverse of an eigenvalue of the linearised RG at the fixed point. It's a property of the fixed point, not of the microscopic model you started from. That already explains why \(\nu\) needn't be a nice fraction: it's whatever the derivative of a complicated map happens to be.

    Many couplings, and universality

    Real coarse-graining generates new couplings: next-nearest-neighbour bonds, four-spin terms, and so on. So the RG really acts on an infinite-dimensional space of couplings \(\{K_1,K_2,\dots\}\). Near a fixed point, linearise as before. You get a matrix \(\partial K'_a/\partial K_b\), and its eigenvectors ("scaling fields") each come with an eigenvalue \(b^{y_i}\).

    Here is the key fact. Typically only a few eigenvalues are relevant. For the Ising fixed point there are exactly two: temperature \(t\) and magnetic field \(h\). All the others, infinitely many, are irrelevant.

    The flow near a fixed point, projected onto one relevant coupling (horizontal) and one irrelevant one (vertical). The critical surface (blue) is the set of all theories that flow into the fixed point. Start slightly off it and you first approach the fixed point, then peel away to one phase or the other.

    The irrelevant directions span the critical surface, the set of all microscopic Hamiltonians that are exactly critical. A real magnet, with its messy crystal structure and longer-range interactions, sits somewhere on the surface when tuned to \(T_c\). So does a liquid at its critical point, once you identify density with magnetisation. Under the flow they all fall into the same fixed point, forgetting everything about where they started except the directions that are relevant.

    Universality in one picture. The exponents are properties of the destination, so every system that arrives there shares them. What decides the destination is coarse information: the dimension of space, the symmetry of the order parameter (up/down here), and whether interactions are short-ranged.

    This is universality. Two systems are in the same universality class when they flow to the same fixed point. Measurements on fluids like xenon and sulfur hexafluoride near their liquid–gas critical points give \(\beta\approx0.32\)–\(0.33\), in line with the 3D Ising value \(0.3265\).

    It also explains why a magnet needs two knobs tuned (temperature and field) to reach criticality: the number of knobs you must tune equals the number of relevant directions.

    All the exponents from two numbers

    The free energy per site \(f\) behaves like a density. After one RG step each block covers \(b^d\) old sites, so the singular part of \(f\) obeys

    \[ f(t,h) = b^{-d}\,f(b^{y_t}t,\ b^{y_h}h). \]

    Differentiating this gives every thermodynamic exponent in terms of \(y_t\) and \(y_h\) (you'll derive \(\beta\) in exercise 3.2):

    \[ \nu=\frac1{y_t},\quad \alpha = 2-\frac{d}{y_t},\quad \beta=\frac{d-y_h}{y_t},\quad \gamma=\frac{2y_h-d}{y_t},\quad \delta=\frac{y_h}{d-y_h},\quad \eta=d+2-2y_h. \]

    Six exponents from two numbers means four relations between them, and these hold identically:

    \[ \alpha+2\beta+\gamma=2\ \ (\text{Rushbrooke}),\qquad \gamma=\beta(\delta-1)\ \ (\text{Widom}),\qquad \gamma=\nu(2-\eta)\ \ (\text{Fisher}),\qquad d\nu=2-\alpha\ \ (\text{Josephson}). \]

    These relations were found in experiments before anyone could explain them. Checking them symbolically:

    al = 2 - d/yt; be = (d - yh)/yt; ga = (2 yh - d)/yt;
de = yh/(d - yh); nu = 1/yt; eta = d + 2 - 2 yh;
Simplify[{al + 2 be + ga - 2, ga - be (de - 1), ga - nu (2 - eta), d nu - (2 - al)}]
(* {0, 0, 0, 0} *)
{nu, al, be, ga, de, eta} /. {d -> 2, yt -> 1, yh -> 15/8}
(* {1, 0, 1/8, 7/4, 15, 1/4}  -- Onsager's exact 2D Ising exponents *)

    For the 2D Ising model, Onsager's exact solution corresponds to \(y_t=1\) and \(y_h=15/8\).

    An approximate recursion in two dimensions

    Can we find the 2D fixed point with a pencil? Not exactly: majority rule in 2D generates infinitely many couplings. The simplest approximation that keeps a single coupling is the Migdal–Kadanoff scheme. On the square lattice with \(b=2\), first move half the bonds onto their neighbours, which leaves a set of one-dimensional chains with doubled coupling \(2K\). Then decimate those chains exactly as in Part 2. The result is

    \[ K' = \operatorname{artanh}\!\big(\tanh^2 2K\big) = \tfrac12\ln\cosh 4K. \]

    Unlike the 1D map, this one crosses the diagonal:

    Starting a hair on either side of \(K^*\), iteration runs away in opposite directions: to the hot fixed point or the cold one. \(K^*\) is an unstable fixed point, and temperature is relevant there. That's a phase transition.

    Solving numerically (Wolfram's FindRoot, cross-checked in Python and JavaScript): \(K^*=0.3047\), \(R'(K^*)=1.6786\), so \(y_t=\log_2 1.6786=0.7472\) and \(\nu=1.338\).

    Compare with the exact answer, \(K_c=\tfrac12\ln(1+\sqrt2)=0.4407\) and \(\nu=1\). The Migdal–Kadanoff scheme gets the structure right (there is an unstable fixed point, temperature is relevant) and the numbers wrong by 30%. That's typical of uncontrolled real-space approximations, and it's the reason Wilson's momentum-space method, with a small parameter to expand in, was such a breakthrough. We build it next.

    Exercises

    🤔 Problem 3.1. Using the Migdal–Kadanoff map \(R(K)=\tfrac12\ln\cosh4K\), show that \(R'(K)=2\tanh 4K\) and so \(y_t = 1+\log_2\tanh(4K^*)\). With \(K^*=0.304689\), evaluate \(y_t\).
    Show solution
    \(R'(K)=\tfrac12\cdot\frac{4\sinh4K}{\cosh4K}=2\tanh4K\). Then \(y_t=\log_2R'(K^*)=1+\log_2\tanh(4K^*)\). With \(4K^*=1.218756\), \(\tanh=0.839287\), so \(y_t=1+\log_2(0.839287)=1-0.25277=0.74724\) and \(\nu=1/y_t=1.338\), matching the value quoted above.

    🤔 Problem 3.2. Starting from \(f(t,h)=b^{-d}f(b^{y_t}t,b^{y_h}h)\), derive \(\beta=(d-y_h)/y_t\). (Hint: \(m=-\partial f/\partial h\) at \(h=0\), then choose \(b\) as in the derivation of \(\nu\).)
    Show solution
    Differentiate both sides with respect to \(h\): \(m(t,h)=b^{-d+y_h}m(b^{y_t}t,b^{y_h}h)\). Set \(h=0\) and choose \(b=|t|^{-1/y_t}\): \(m(t,0)=|t|^{(d-y_h)/y_t}m(\pm1,0)\). Below \(T_c\), \(m\propto|t|^\beta\), so \(\beta=(d-y_h)/y_t\). For 2D Ising, \((2-15/8)/1=1/8\).

    🤔 Problem 3.3. A liquid–gas critical point is reached by tuning temperature and pressure. Why exactly two knobs? What plays the role of the magnetic field?
    Show solution
    The Ising fixed point has two relevant directions, so a generic system needs two parameters tuned to land on the critical surface. For a fluid the order parameter is \(\rho-\rho_c\), the deviation of density from its critical value. The "field" conjugate to it is the deviation of the chemical potential (equivalently pressure) from its value on the coexistence curve. Tuning pressure to that curve sets \(h=0\), and tuning temperature to \(T_c\) sets \(t=0\). The asymmetry between liquid and gas is an irrelevant coupling, which is why the fluid still lands in the Ising class.

    🤔 Problem 3.4. In the widget, find the temperature where the third blocked panel stops looking like the original. Explain why this temperature moves towards \(T_c\) as you add more blocking steps.
    Show solution
    After \(n\) steps you are looking at scale \(2^n\). The blocked picture looks critical as long as \(2^n\lesssim\xi(t)\propto|t|^{-\nu}\), since up to the correlation length the system can't tell it's off criticality. So you notice the deviation when \(|t|\sim2^{-n/\nu}=2^{-n}\) in 2D (\(\nu=1\)). Each extra blocking step halves the window around \(T_c\) in which the picture still looks critical. On a 128×128 lattice the window can't shrink below \(|t|\sim1/128\), which is why the widget stays critical-looking in a small band.
