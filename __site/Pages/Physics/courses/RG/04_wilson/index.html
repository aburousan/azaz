    Renormalisation Group IV — Wilson's Momentum Shells

    Part 4 of seven. previous: Fixed points & universality · Course home · next: Wilson–Fisher

    Real-space blocking gave us the right ideas and poor numbers. Wilson's move was to coarse-grain in momentum space instead, where "throw away short-distance detail" becomes "integrate out high-momentum modes", and where perturbation theory gives controlled approximations. This part sets up the machinery on a free field, where everything is exact. The next part turns on the interaction.

    From spins to a field

    Block-spin an Ising magnet enough times and each block spin averages over thousands of original spins. It's then better described as a continuous variable, the local magnetisation \(\phi(\mathbf x)\). What's the most general action for it? Landau and Ginzburg's answer: keep everything allowed by symmetry, expand in powers of \(\phi\) and its gradients, and stop at low order. The Ising symmetry \(\phi\to-\phi\) kills odd powers, leaving

    \[ S[\phi] = \int\mathrm d^dx\left[\tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 + \tfrac12 r\,\phi^2 + \frac{u}{4!}\phi^4\right], \]

    with \(r\propto T-T_0\) changing sign near the transition. The partition function is \(Z=\int\mathcal D\phi\,e^{-S[\phi]}\).

    This is exactly the Euclidean \(\phi^4\) theory from Part 1, with \(r=m^2\) and \(u=\lambda\). A statistical field theory in \(d\) dimensions and a quantum field theory in \(d\) spacetime dimensions are the same mathematical object. That's why a course on critical magnets is also a course on QFT.

    The underlying lattice spacing \(a\) means there are no modes with wavelength shorter than \(a\). In momentum space, the field only has components with \(|\mathbf k|<\Lambda\sim\pi/a\). That's the cutoff, and here it's physical.

    One RG step in three moves

    Split every mode into slow and fast:

    \[ \phi(\mathbf x) = \phi_<(\mathbf x) + \phi_>(\mathbf x), \qquad \phi_<:\ |\mathbf k|<\Lambda/b,\qquad \phi_>:\ \Lambda/b<|\mathbf k|<\Lambda. \]

    A Wilsonian RG step. Integrate out the fast modes in the shell, stretch momenta so the cutoff is back at \(\Lambda\), and rescale the field so the kinetic term keeps its standard form. What changes are the couplings.

    1. Coarse-grain. Integrate out the shell. This defines an action for the slow modes alone:

    \[ e^{-S'[\phi_<]} = \int\mathcal D\phi_>\,e^{-S[\phi_<+\phi_>]}. \]

    Any question about long-distance physics (correlations of \(\phi_<\)) gets the same answer from \(S'\) as from \(S\). We've changed the description, not the physics.

    2. Rescale lengths. The new theory has cutoff \(\Lambda/b\). To compare it with the old one, stretch: \(\mathbf k'=b\mathbf k\) (equivalently \(\mathbf x'=\mathbf x/b\)), which puts the cutoff back at \(\Lambda\). This is the zoom-out step, the same as viewing the blocked lattice with the new, bigger spacing as "one unit".

    3. Rescale the field. Choose \(\phi'=\zeta^{-1}\phi_<\) with \(\zeta\) picked to keep the coefficient of \(\tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2\) equal to one. Without this there'd be no fixed point to find, since the kinetic term would just drift.

    After the three steps the action has the same form, with new couplings \((r',u',\dots)\). That's the RG map, now for a field.

    The Gaussian fixed point, exactly

    Switch off the interaction, \(u=0\). In momentum space the action is a sum over independent modes,

    \[ S = \int_{|\mathbf k|<\Lambda}\frac{\mathrm d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}\,\tfrac12(k^2+r)\,|\phi(\mathbf k)|^2, \]

    so step 1 is trivial: the fast modes decouple and their integral is just a constant. Steps 2 and 3: put \(\mathbf k=\mathbf k'/b\), so \(\mathrm d^dk=b^{-d}\mathrm d^dk'\) and \(k^2=b^{-2}k'^2\), and set \(\phi_<(\mathbf k'/b)=\zeta\,\phi'(\mathbf k')\):

    \[ S' = \int_{|\mathbf k'|<\Lambda}\frac{\mathrm d^dk'}{(2\pi)^d}\,\tfrac12\, b^{-d}\zeta^2\big(b^{-2}k'^2 + r\big)|\phi'(\mathbf k')|^2. \]

    Keeping the \(k'^2\) coefficient fixed needs \(\zeta^2=b^{d+2}\), and then

    \[ \boxed{\; r' = b^2 r.\;} \]

    The point \(r=0\) is a fixed point: the free massless field, called the Gaussian fixed point. It has one relevant direction, \(r\), with \(y_r=2\), and so \(\nu=1/y_r=\tfrac12\). This is exactly the mean-field (Landau) value. It also matches the free propagator, \(G(k)=1/(k^2+r)\), which decays over a length \(\xi=r^{-1/2}\propto|t|^{-1/2}\).

    Scaling dimensions: power counting is an RG statement

    In real space the same rescaling reads \(\mathbf x'=\mathbf x/b\) and \(\phi'(\mathbf x')=b^{(d-2)/2}\phi(\mathbf x)\) (exercise 4.1). The exponent \((d-2)/2\) is the scaling dimension of \(\phi\) at the Gaussian fixed point. It's the mass dimension \([\phi]\) from Part 1, reappearing as an RG eigenvalue.

    Now add any local term \(g_n\int\mathrm d^dx\,\phi^n\) and ask how \(g_n\) transforms at tree level: \(\mathrm d^dx=b^d\mathrm d^dx'\) and \(\phi^n=b^{-n(d-2)/2}\phi'^n\), so

    \[ g_n' = b^{\,y_n}g_n,\qquad y_n = d-n\,\frac{d-2}{2} = [g_n]. \]

    The RG eigenvalue of a coupling near the Gaussian fixed point is simply its mass dimension. Positive mass dimension means relevant, zero means marginal, negative means irrelevant. The same holds for couplings with derivatives: each derivative costs one unit.

    Power counting in \(d=4\). The quartic coupling sits exactly on the fence: marginal. Everything with more fields or more derivatives is irrelevant.

    This is why you only ever write a few terms in a Lagrangian. An irrelevant coupling \(g\) with \([g]=-p\) contributes to low-energy processes in the combination \(g E^p\), suppressed by \((E/\Lambda)^p\) if \(g\sim\Lambda^{-p}\). By the time you coarse-grain down to the scales you measure at, only the relevant and marginal couplings survive. The theory you see at low energy is forced to be simple. "Renormalisable" theories, the ones with only non-negative-dimension couplings, aren't a lucky choice nature made. They're what any theory looks like from far away. Part 7 returns to this.

    Why four dimensions is special

    Apply the formula to the quartic coupling: \(y_4=4-d\).

    • \(d>4\): \(u\) is irrelevant at the Gaussian fixed point. A critical system flows to the free theory, and mean-field exponents (\(\nu=\tfrac12\) and friends) are exact.

    • \(d=4\): \(u\) is marginal. Tree level can't decide; loops will (Part 5 shows it's marginally irrelevant). Mean-field exponents survive, with logarithmic corrections.

    • \(d<4\): \(u\) is relevant. The Gaussian fixed point now has an extra unstable direction, so a critical system can't flow into it. It has to flow somewhere else, to a fixed point with \(u^*\neq0\). The exponents there differ from mean field. That's why \(\nu\) for 3D magnets is \(0.63\), not \(0.5\).

    Four is the upper critical dimension of the Ising class. Wilson and Fisher's idea was to work in \(d=4-\epsilon\), where the new fixed point sits at a small coupling \(u^*=O(\epsilon)\) and perturbation theory controls it. That's the next part.

    📝 Note If you're reading Melo's notes alongside this course, they use dimensionless couplings \(g_2=m^2/\Lambda^2\) and \(g_4=\lambda\Lambda^{d-4}\), and write the flow as \(\Lambda\,\mathrm d g/\mathrm d\Lambda\). Lowering \(\Lambda\) is flowing to the infrared, so their \(\Lambda\,\mathrm d/\mathrm d\Lambda\) is minus our \(\mathrm d/\mathrm d\ell\) with \(b=e^{\ell}\). The Gaussian result above reads \(\Lambda\,\mathrm d g_2/\mathrm d\Lambda=-2g_2\) there.

    Exercises

    🤔 Problem 4.1. Show that the Fourier-space rescaling \(\phi_<(\mathbf k'/b)=\zeta\,\phi'(\mathbf k')\) with \(\zeta^2=b^{d+2}\) corresponds to \(\phi'(\mathbf x')=b^{(d-2)/2}\phi_<(\mathbf x)\) in real space, with \(\mathbf x'=\mathbf x/b\). Use \(\phi(\mathbf x)=\int\frac{\mathrm d^dk}{(2\pi)^d}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\phi(\mathbf k)\).
    Show solution
    \(\phi'(\mathbf x')=\int\frac{\mathrm d^dk'}{(2\pi)^d}e^{i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'}\zeta^{-1}\phi_<(\mathbf k'/b)\). Substitute \(\mathbf k'=b\mathbf k\): \(\mathrm d^dk'=b^d\mathrm d^dk\) and \(\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf x'=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x\), so \(\phi'(\mathbf x')=b^d\zeta^{-1}\phi_<(\mathbf x)=b^{d-(d+2)/2}\phi_<(\mathbf x)=b^{(d-2)/2}\phi_<(\mathbf x)\).

    🤔 Problem 4.2. Classify \(\phi^2,\phi^4,\phi^6\) as relevant, marginal or irrelevant at the Gaussian fixed point in \(d=3\). In which dimension is a \(\phi^3\) coupling marginal?
    Show solution
    In \(d=3\), \([\phi]=\tfrac12\), so \(y_n=3-n/2\): \(y_2=2\) (relevant), \(y_4=1\) (relevant), \(y_6=0\) (marginal). For \(\phi^3\): \(y_3=d-3(d-2)/2=(6-d)/2\), which vanishes at \(d=6\). That's why \(\phi^3\) theory in six dimensions is the standard toy model for asymptotic freedom and for percolation.

    🤔 Problem 4.3. Find the RG eigenvalue of the coupling of \((\nabla\phi)^2\phi^2\) at the Gaussian fixed point in general \(d\). Is it ever relevant for \(d\geq2\)?
    Show solution
    The operator has dimension \(2+4\cdot\frac{d-2}{2}=2d-2\), so \(y=d-(2d-2)=2-d\). It's marginal in \(d=2\) and irrelevant for all \(d>2\). In \(d=4\), \(y=-2\), as in the figure.

    🤔 Problem 4.4. Suppose a theory has an irrelevant coupling \(g_6\) with \([g_6]=-2\), and naturally \(g_6\sim c/\Lambda^2\) with \(c\sim1\). By roughly how much is its effect on a process at energy \(E=100\) GeV suppressed if \(\Lambda=10\) TeV? If \(\Lambda\) were the Planck scale?
    Show solution
    The effect enters as \(g_6E^2\sim(E/\Lambda)^2\). For \(\Lambda=10\) TeV that's \((0.01)^2=10^{-4}\): small, but within reach of precision experiments, which is how colliders hunt for new physics indirectly. For \(\Lambda=1.2\times10^{19}\) GeV it's about \(7\times10^{-35}\): unmeasurable. This is why the Standard Model can be tested in exquisite detail without knowing anything about quantum gravity.
